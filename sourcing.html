Sourcing and Producers

Where it comes from

Italy does not cook as one country and it does not sell as one either. An oil from Puglia and an oil from Liguria are not two grades of the same thing — they are two different ideas about what oil is for. So we buy region by region, and we say which region.

Eight regions we buy across

  • 1

    Piemonte

    Truffles · Rice · Hazelnut · Nebbiolo

    In autumn the white truffle of Alba sets the calendar for the whole house. Piedmont is also where we buy rice that holds its centre through twenty minutes of stirring, the hazelnut that ruined every other hazelnut for us, and Nebbiolo from growers in Barolo and Barbaresco.

  • 2

    Veneto

    Amarone · Prosecco · Rice · Radicchio

    The Valpolicella dries its Corvina on racks until the wine turns into something closer to a conclusion than a drink. From the same region: Vialone Nano for risotto, and the bitter winter chicories of Treviso and Castelfranco.

  • 3

    Emilia-Romagna

    Parmigiano Reggiano · Prosciutto · Balsamico

    If a single province had to answer for the Italian larder, it would be this one. We buy Parmigiano by lot from dairies in Reggio and Modena, hams from the hills above Parma, and balsamic from families whose batteries of casks are older than the country.

  • 4

    Toscana

    Olive oil · Pecorino · Finocchiona · Sangiovese

    Tuscan oil is picked green and early, which is why it tastes of pepper and artichoke and why it costs what it costs. With it come the sheep cheeses of the Crete Senesi, fennel-scented salumi, and Sangiovese from Montalcino and Chianti Classico.

  • 5

    Campania

    San Marzano · Bufala · Gragnano pasta · Lemon

    Volcanic soil under the Sarno valley grows the only tomato we will put our name to. Nearby, buffalo mozzarella made in the morning and eaten the same day, pasta dried in the sea air at Gragnano, and the lemons of the Amalfi coast.

  • 6

    Puglia

    Olive oil · Burrata · Durum wheat · Primitivo

    The heel presses more oil than any other region in Italy, from groves where individual trees predate the Roman roads beside them. Puglia also gives us burrata, the durum that goes into our dried pasta, and the deep reds of Manduria and Salice Salentino.

  • 7

    Calabria

    Chilli · Soppressata · Bergamot · Tropea onion

    The one part of Italy that cooks with real heat. Calabrian chilli goes into the cured meats as much as onto the table, and the coast around Tropea grows a sweet red onion and a bergamot that exists almost nowhere else.

  • 8

    Sicilia

    Pistachio · Capers · Sea salt · Etna wine

    Three coastlines and a volcano. Bronte pistachio grown on lava, capers from Pantelleria, anchovies and salt from Trapani, and the reds coming off Etna that have rearranged how people think about Sicilian wine.

Denominations

What the letters actually promise

Protected-origin marks get used as decoration often enough that it is worth saying plainly what they mean. They are not quality medals awarded by a jury. They are enforceable rules about where a food may be made, from what, and by which method — audited by a consortium, with the right to the name withdrawn if the rules are broken.

Not everything worth importing carries one. Some of the best work we see comes from producers too small or too stubborn to sit inside a consortium, and we buy from them on the evidence of the thing itself. But where a denomination exists, we will tell you whether what you are buying holds it.

DOP

Denominazione di Origine Protetta. Every stage — growing, making, ageing — happens inside a defined zone, to a defined method. The strictest of the three.

IGP

Indicazione Geografica Protetta. At least one stage of production is tied to the region, and the region is the reason the food is what it is.

DOCG

Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita. Wine only, and the top tier of it: zone, grape, yield and minimum ageing all fixed, with bottles government-sealed.

Provenance

Ask us where anything came from

We can name the producer, the zone and the season behind everything we carry. If we cannot, we do not carry it.
