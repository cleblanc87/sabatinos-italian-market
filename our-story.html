Our Story

A buying house, not a warehouse

Most imported Italian food in this country passes through four or five sets of hands before it reaches a kitchen, and loses something at every one of them. Sabatino’s exists to remove those hands.

How We Began

It began as a complaint

Anyone who has cooked in Italy and then tried to cook the same thing here knows the feeling. The label says the right region. The tin says the right tomato. And what comes out of it is a polite imitation of something you remember being extraordinary.

The reason is rarely fraud. It is distance, and time, and the number of intermediaries who each need the shipment to be a little cheaper and a little more durable than the one before. Oil sits in clear glass under a warehouse skylight. Cheese is cut and wrapped months before anyone eats it. A ham is rushed out of the cellar because the cellar is needed for the next one.

So we built the company backwards from the kitchen. We buy in Italy ourselves, from the people who actually make the thing. We move it under refrigeration on the shortest route we can arrange. And we keep the paperwork that says where every crate came from, because provenance you cannot document is just a story.

We would rather carry eleven things we can account for than four hundred we cannot. The House of Sabatino
Our Standards

What has to be true before we buy

Three tests, applied in order. A producer who fails the first is never asked about the second.

I — Selection

We have tasted this lot

Not the product — the lot. We taste against the previous season and against the best comparable house we know, and we buy the batch rather than the brand. A dairy that was extraordinary in March can be ordinary in September, and the label will not tell you which one you have.

II — Provenance

We can document it

Zone, producer, method, season, and any denomination it holds — on file before the first case ships, and available to you on request. Where a consortium certifies the food, we keep the certification. Where none exists, we keep our own notes from the visit.

III — Handling

It survives the journey

A good cheese badly shipped is a bad cheese. We agree the cold chain, the packing and the route before we agree the price, and we would rather delay a consignment than send it a way we know will cost it something.

The Sourcing Calendar

Our year is the Italian year

We do not buy everything all the time, because Italy does not make everything all the time. This is roughly where we are, and when.

  1. September to November

    La Raccolta

    The olive harvest. We are in Puglia and Tuscany at the mills while the fruit is still coming in, tasting oil that is hours old and deciding on the spot, because the best lots are gone within days of pressing.

  2. October to December

    Alba

    White truffle season in Piedmont. Allocation is daily and weather-dependent, and no one who tells you otherwise is being straight with you.

  3. January to March

    The Cellars

    Quiet months, spent underground. Ham and salumi selection for the year ahead, cheese lots marked for the summer, and the black truffle of Norcia at its best.

  4. April to June

    The Tasting Table

    New-season oil lands and goes out to the kitchens that waited for it. We taste back through the winter’s cheese and confirm or replace every house on the list.

  5. July to September

    Conserve

    The jarring season. Tomatoes in the Sarno valley, capers on Pantelleria, anchovies at Trapani — all of it put up within hours of picking, which is the entire difference between a conserve and a compromise.
